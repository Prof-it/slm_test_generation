# `threat_to_validity.md`
*   **benchmark quality**: The chosen benchmark, TestEval, may not fully capture the real-world performance of the models. While repository-level benchmarks like TestGenEval could offer higher fidelity, their application was precluded by the context window limitations of the Small Language Models (SLMs) under evaluation and the computational constraints of this study. The scarcity of alternative test generation benchmarks is a known challenge in the field. To partially mitigate this, we conducted an internal "dogfooding" analysis to provide a practical performance assessment.
*   **benchmark leaking**: Models released subsequent to the initial publication of the TestEval dataset are susceptible to data contamination, as they may have been trained on its contents. This potential for benchmark leakage is addressed by supplementing our quantitative results with the aforementioned "dogfooding" experiment, which uses a private, unseen dataset.
*   **model fault probability**: To serve SLMs in the 7–8B range, we utilized VLM with AWQ. However, most LLM providers do not offer AWQ-quantized versions of their models, which forces us to rely on third-party AWQ weight providers such as `hugging-quants`, `TheBloke`, `jakiAJK` and `TechxGenus`. Although these providers are trusted and well known, using unofficial quantizations introduces the risk of faulty or inconsistent model weights. This highlights the need for official AWQ-quantized weights from the original LLM providers.
*   **prompt bias**: Our evaluation utilizes a single, standardized prompt. This formulation may inadvertently favor certain models, introducing a potential bias. A more robust methodology would involve experimenting with multiple prompt variations; however, this was not feasible due to computational limitations.
*   **two-step prompt bias**: Following the original TestEval methodology, we employed a two-step prompting strategy. The specific structure and objectives of this multi-step process may introduce confounding variables that affect model performance. A detailed analysis of this potential bias was beyond the scope of this work due to resource constraints.
*   **pass@1 bias**: Given the non-deterministic nature of SLMs, evaluation metrics such as pass@k (where k > 1) are often used to provide a more stable measure of performance by averaging over multiple generation attempts. Due to computational constraints, our study is restricted to a pass@1 metric, which evaluates only a single output per test case.
*   **inference parameter bias**: The study primarily utilizes default inference parameters, which may not be optimal for every model. To investigate the impact of this choice, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by varying the temperature parameter, testing both a conservative value (0.2) and a more creative one (0.8).
*   **post processing bias**: The post-processing pipeline was intentionally designed to be minimal to reflect the models' raw generative capabilities. We acknowledge that more sophisticated or aggressive algorithmic post-processing could artificially inflate the reported pass rates. Our approach prioritizes a more authentic representation of model accuracy.

## Evaluation Scope Limitations
*   **High Computational Cost**: Mutation testing was excluded due to its prohibitive computational requirements. As established by Jia and Harman (2011), mutation testing is computationally expensive because it requires executing the entire test suite against massive numbers of mutant versions of the code. In the context of LLM-generated code, Siddiq et al. (2025) further highlight that validation of generated tests on real-world functions already incurs significant overhead, which mutation testing would exacerbate beyond the 20-week timeline of this project.
*   **Benchmark Validity:** We acknowledge the risks of relying solely on synthetic benchmarks like TestEval. To mitigate this, we included an "Out-of-Band" validation set using real-world open-source functions, addressing concerns raised by [].
